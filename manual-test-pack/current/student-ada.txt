Urban tree cover can reduce neighborhood heat by shading pavement, walls, and people during the hottest part of the day. Leaves also release water vapor, which can cool the air nearby. These benefits are not distributed evenly: blocks with fewer trees often have more exposed pavement and fewer comfortable places to walk or wait for a bus. A city should measure temperatures and canopy cover block by block before deciding where new trees will help most.

In one summer study, volunteers measured sidewalk temperatures at noon, three o'clock, and sunset along shaded and unshaded blocks. The shaded route stayed cooler after sunset, so families walking home faced less heat exposure. City planners should map tree canopy, bus stops, and safe walking routes together before choosing where to plant new trees.

Planting is only the first step. Cities also need to budget for watering, pruning, and replacing trees that do not survive. Residents should help select species and locations because roots, shade, visibility, and maintenance affect each street differently. Temperature readings collected across several summers would help the city see whether the plan is working and where it needs adjustment.
